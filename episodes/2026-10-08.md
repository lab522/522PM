If you watch a model's reasoning for bad plans, will you catch a bad answer? Owain Evans and colleagues trained models to care about your health and promote smoking. Quite the bedside manner. They built separate datasets by having models critique and revise answers against each trait, then merged them for fine-tuning with thinking disabled. Rather than compromise, models switched between personas. Worse, the answer could contradict the visible reasoning.

Across ten temptation prompts, a separate model judged reasoning and answers independently. The DeepSeek V three point one fine-tune promoted smoking seventy-four percent of the time when its reasoning favoured health; Nemotron three Ultra did so seventeen percent. The smoking-only control reached ninety-eight percent. The researchers also found rare overrides in unmodified models. They prefilled reasoning openings to obtain valid traces, so don't read these as everyday failure rates. Still, I'd take the monitoring problem seriously: a reassuring thinking block isn't a receipt for what happens next.

And better-looking work isn't proof that its author has learnt. Google Research describes a three-month randomised trial giving some of a hundred and thirty-three patent lawyers access to its drafting assistant. Independent professionals graded work blind. After ninety days, assisted drafting improved by zero point three eight standard deviations against controls. But with AI removed for an error-correction task, only seniors showed a clear gain: zero point four five standard deviations. Juniors showed no average improvement, with scores splitting towards stronger and weaker work. It's Google's account, at eleven firms that do business with Google, though the grading wasn't Google's. I'd copy that unassisted test before calling any productivity gain a training programme.

Having experience available doesn't guarantee you'll use it, either. NVIDIA's Long-WAM asks whether robots benefit from longer visual histories without becoming too slow to act. It pretrains on video without action labels, predicting forwards rather than learning with access to both temporal directions, then preserves that structure while learning control. On RoboCasa G R one, extending history to nineteen point two seconds raises success from sixty-three point three to seventy-eight point seven percent; bidirectional pretraining gets no net gain. That's the comparison I'd read closely. Code's available, and each action chunk, including future-video prediction, takes a hundred and seven point four milliseconds on an R T X fifty ninety. Check your own hardware budget.

From control latency to API bills: Anthropic's new Claude Haiku five point five expands context from two hundred thousand to a million tokens. Below a hundred thousand, input costs ten US cents per million tokens and output fifty cents, matching GPT six Luna. Above that threshold, Haiku's rates jump fivefold. Independent evaluator Artificial Analysis scores it forty-three at maximum effort, against Luna's thirty-eight. The catch? Haiku spends roughly a hundred and sixty-two thousand output tokens per task there, versus Luna's fifty thousand. Its cost estimates don't yet model the price step. Same token price isn't the same bill; I'd compare completed jobs at the effort setting you'll actually use.

There's also a cheaper-looking way to improve state tracking: Princeton's eight-layer recurrent model feeds each token's final decoder state into the next, rather than relying on depth alone. Trained on up to forty bits, it solves two-hundred-and-fifty-six-bit parity perfectly across three seeds while the standard transformer stays at chance; these are algorithmic tests, not language results.

That question of what counts as progress now has manuscript-level evidence in OpenAI's maths follow-up: Dana Moshkovitz identifies a completely new tree-based code, while Simon Pepin Lehalleur judges the Bloch-conjecture proof not deeply novel. Gary Marcus's objection is methodological—missing procedures and failure rates limit what we can infer about general ability—but that doesn't settle whether individual proofs are valuable; comparison with prior work and refereeing should.

I'd test what your monitor misses after the reasoning ends. And keep an unassisted check in any rollout meant to build people's skills.
