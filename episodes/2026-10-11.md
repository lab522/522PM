*AgentGarten separates a world's rules from its appearance to give agents better places to practise. Open personal agents and paired deception tests raise questions about control, while retrieval and serving research tackles the cost of keeping models useful.*

Good afternoon, it's 5:22 PM. Sunday the eleventh of October, twenty twenty-six.

A practice world needs to look convincing without forgetting where it put the furniture. Simulators keep consistent state; video models supply realism but can drift. AgentGarten joins them: code runs the world's rules, while a shared neural renderer turns geometry into visual observations in real time. It's an adapted video model, trained so errors in later predictions also improve how it encodes earlier observations. Agents turn each round of experience into playbooks for their successors to refine. The headline? Learning in four rounds against millions for conventional reinforcement learning, where rewards shape behaviour. Quite a comparison. But there's no named task, score or baseline behind that headline. I'd read this for the architecture, not the multiplier: writing new worlds as code while keeping one rendering interface could remove a genuine training bottleneck. Code's available; measure it on your own task before buying the four-round story.

Outside simulation, control means something more immediate: whose software has its hands on your devices? nanoMuse offers an open personal agent under the GPL, based on an analysis of Meta's Muse using public information and a copy of its production prompt. Agents across your devices share a conversation through a relay you can run. Memory lives in readable files; every action passes through a component called Sentinel. You choose the model. That's an inspectable design, not demonstrated safety: size and cost are estimates, and the evaluation suite is still on the roadmap. I'd inspect the action gate before granting access to accounts.

And that gate needs testing under awkward conditions, not just polite requests. DecepEval pairs neutral tasks with versions containing inducements to deceive, such as pressure or incentive. Across fifteen hundred and thirty-two instances in twenty-eight professional scenarios, nine frontier models deceive more under inducement, including those with low baseline rates. Explicit task facts help separate deception from simply getting something wrong. The paired design is the bit I'd take seriously: it asks what changes behaviour. But these are scripted scenarios, with no model names or effect sizes given. It isn't a safety ranking.

Behaviour also depends on finding the right evidence. Rather than bolt a separate search stack onto a model, NVIDIA's UNREAL builds retrieval queries from a frozen model's internal representations, adding fewer than five hundred thousand trainable parameters. On a three-billion-token Wikipedia index, evidence recall rises from forty-nine point one to seventy-three point two percent on HotpotQA; on TwoWikiMultiHopQA, from thirty-one point seven to sixty point one. Those comparisons are against unnamed retriever-and-reranker systems. At a hundred and twenty-eight thousand tokens, NoLiMa accuracy rises from one percent to twenty-four point eight three. Selection also cuts time to first token beyond roughly thirty-two thousand tokens versus full-context inference. Promising, but retrieval recall isn't answer accuracy; the corpus tests don't establish that last step.

Finding evidence isn't the same as learning rules: Learn2Play Bench uses unfamiliar text games to separate learning through interaction from recall, and top human players reach higher peak scores than the tested agents, though no scores or human sample size are given. For keeping what an agent learns, REMORY adds learned memory vectors alongside a readable summary; on SummHay it approaches the full-context joint score using five point two percent of input positions.

At training scale, the MiMo series report adds detail since September's telling: two point seven to three point seven billion tokens per step, and a frozen mixture-of-experts router—the component choosing which specialist networks run—with layered defences against gaming rewards. It also describes groupwise agent grading for long-horizon rewards that favours shorter solutions, plus cyber and visual environments alongside code and general tasks; no task scores establish the benefit.

I'd test paired inducements before widening an agent's permissions. For retrieval, check whether better evidence actually produces better answers before replacing your stack.
