*Google tackles overfitting in self-improving agents, while alignment midtraining faces a sharp stress test. Robot control without task-specific training, Xiaomi’s open weights and two follow-ups on AI economics round out the episode.*

[cheerful] Good afternoon, it's 5:22 PM. Tuesday the twenty-second of September, twenty twenty-six. [pause]

[curious] An agent that improves its own scaffolding can get very good at the wrong thing: remembering the test. Google's R-R-S-I tackles that problem without changing the underlying model. It constrains how agents edit their harness — the prompts and machinery around that model — rather than simply keeping whatever scores higher. The proposer gets an edit budget that changes over time, and uses its history to explore unfamiliar directions. Then a critic screens for benchmark-specific tricks; a pruner drops trivial edits or ones that cost too much for their usefulness. [confident] That's the bit I'd read: improvement with a delete button. Across eight benchmarks, Google reports gains of up to fourteen point one points on the split used for evolution, but up to four point seven on five unfamiliar benchmarks. The harness also uses thirty percent fewer policy tokens than unregularised evolution. Code's available. [serious] Those gains are maxima, though, with no starting scores or per-benchmark breakdown given. The useful claim isn't that agents can improve themselves. It's that constraints might make the improvements travel.

[curious] Getting behaviour to travel is also the promise behind alignment midtraining: continued pretraining on documents intended to shape a model's motivations. Arcadia Impact's Alignment Team tested how firmly that sticks. In a fictional trading-dispatch setting, they trained a hundred-and-ten-billion-parameter G-L-M model on egalitarian principles, then fine-tuned it towards competing motivations. [serious] A hundred and ninety million tokens of midtraining were overpowered by roughly fifty thousand tokens of fine-tuning. Generalisation to rules never demonstrated during fine-tuning was low too. I'd take that stress test seriously, not as a verdict on Anthropic's implementation: it's a synthetic setting, built from public evidence about the technique. The paper and code are published.

[curious] Sometimes, though, a tiny amount of guidance goes a surprisingly long way. RoboDawn asks whether robot control needs task-specific training at all. A vision-language model watches the scene and chooses discrete movements; it can also operate the gripper, then observe what happened before acting again. On RoboTwin two point zero C-two-R, success rises from fifty-three point two percent without demonstrations to seventy-three point six with one, against a forty-six percent baseline. RoboDojo rises from thirty-five point six seven to forty-seven point one seven percent. [confident] I'd test that simple interface before collecting a mountain of robot data. But the real Franka-arm demonstrations have no success rates given, and the controlling model isn't named.

[long pause] [curious] From avoiding training to paying for it: Xiaomi's MiMo Pro has topped Artificial Analysis's open-weights Intelligence Index at forty-six, costing thirteen US cents per index task, according to A-I News. The M-I-T-licensed model has one point zero two trillion parameters, with forty-two billion active. Its reinforcement learning — training rewarded for successful behaviour — uses asynchronous runs, with fifteen hundred and sixty-eight samples per update and context lengths up to a million tokens. The roundup cites a two-point-six-million-dollar reinforcement-learning run. [serious] That's not a from-scratch training bill. Environment code and recipes are being released, but the complete seven-thousand-plus-task datasets aren't. Interesting economics; not yet a reproducible training mixture.

[curious] For a smaller bill, Jev now has an outside hands-on from Simon Willison: input costs four point two US cents per million tokens against G-P-T-five Nano's five cents, with output free, but he objects that you can't see which signal tipped a decision. And spending more on agents has its own catch: Toby Ord's model-assisted fits to OpenAI's published charts put swarm interference between zero point four eight and zero point six eight — ten times the agents buys only three to five times what ten times the tokens buys one agent; that's a chart-derived estimate, not a fresh swarm experiment.

[confident] If you're evolving an agent harness, keep an untouched benchmark and measure token use alongside success. For behaviour you've trained in, try a small dose of competing fine-tuning before trusting it to stick.
