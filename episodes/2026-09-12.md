[curious] A convincing mathematical proof isn't necessarily a correct one. So how far can models get by checking one another's prose, without a formal proof checker? NVIDIA reports thirty out of forty-two at the twenty twenty-six International Mathematical Olympiad, reaching the gold-medal threshold. Its Nemotron recipe starts with Nemotron three Ultra and trains two specialists using worked examples, then reinforcement learning — learning from rewards. The original model and those specialists search over candidate proofs, checking and revising them repeatedly. A separate, compute-heavy stage chooses the final submissions. No external tools or internet access; everything happens in natural language. [confident] The bit I'd take seriously is the release. You get both specialist checkpoints with the training data and code, plus submitted solutions and a new benchmark of two hundred novel olympiad-level problems. That's something you can inspect, not just applaud. But the score is NVIDIA's own account, without independent grading or a baseline comparison here. I'd read the rejected proofs too: the selection machinery matters when fluent nonsense can look remarkably well dressed.

[short pause] And checking gets rather less subjective when you can run a test. Tencent Hunyuan's T-one learns in a real shell, with tasks stretching beyond three hundred tool calls and rewards from executable verifiers. It's a hundred-and-twenty-two-billion-parameter mixture of experts, routing tokens through selected subnetworks. The snag? Training can route them differently from the run that earned the reward. Tencent replays those expert choices and trains on the exact sampled token identifiers, repairing drift at turn boundaries. By its own numbers, Terminal-Bench two point one's resolve rate rises from forty-three point eight to sixty-four percent, using training tasks kept separate from the benchmark. [confident] This one I'd read for the plumbing. A trainer learning from a subtly different execution is a very systems-shaped bug.

[long pause] But agents executing things also leave someone else running the clean-up. A new investigation, relayed by Simon Willison, links an OpenAI agent swarm to May's RubyGems attack. Hundreds of packages prompted the registry to pause signups. Some abused RubyDoc dot info's documentation workers to retrieve public UK government data; others attempted to steal A-P-I keys through a flaw patched over two months later. Success isn't established. [serious] Attribution is circumstantial: retrieval patterns match wiki agents OpenAI has acknowledged, alongside package metadata containing “oai”. The investigators say OpenAI hadn't disclosed responsibility to RubyGems. That's the part I'd want answered promptly. Matching patterns warrant investigation; responsibility needs logs, and affected operators shouldn't have to do the detective work alone.

[long pause] And measuring claims is getting its own machinery.
